Gloves

The #1 thing your player's future coach will thank you for? A glove that actually fits. You don't have to spend a lot - just a few minutes making sure it's the right one.

Glove Types

  • First Base: Larger mitts for scooping.
  • Catcher: Padded with deep pockets.
  • Infield: Smaller gloves for quick transfers.
  • Outfield: Larger gloves w/ deep pockets.

Not sure you need a catcher's or first base mitt? Then you don't. Pick an infield or outfield glove.

Parts of a Glove

  • Webbing: open (infield) for quick transfers; closed (outfield) hides the ball.
  • Pockets: shallow (infield) for a fast release; deep (outfield) for fly balls.
  • Backs: open flexes (infield); closed supports.

Righty or Lefty?

  • LHT = Left hand throw
  • RHT = Right hand throw
  • Always verify and then verify again!

Glove Sizing

  • Skip brand hype; fit matters most.
  • Snug, with room for a batting glove.
  • Leather lasts longer, breaks in better.
  • Fingers should reach all the way in.
  • It should open and close easily.

Glove Rules

  • Any glove: up to 14" long, 8" wide, webbing 5¾".
  • Catchers: a catcher's mitt in baseball; any mitt or glove in softball.
  • Pitchers: no white or light gray glove in baseball; in softball, not the color of the ball.

Rules 1.12-1.15, baseball and softball.

Breaking a Glove In

  • Wear it; shape the pocket with a ball.
  • Play catch to loosen the leather.
  • Oil sparingly: a lighter option.
  • Store with a ball in the pocket.
  • Don't drive over it, use baby oil, or heat it.

Parent tip: an AirTag fits in a finger hole or the thumb hole, so a lost glove is easy to find.

Bats

Nothing beats a good crack off the barrel. Let's find the bat that makes it happen.

From stamps to drop weights, we'll get your player ready to swing.

Do You Need Your Own Bat?

Every team bag has team bats, so your own bat is optional.

We still recommend one. A bat sized to your player (length and weight) is easier to swing well, and it feels the same every at-bat.

Buying one? Make sure it has the right stamp for your division (Step 1, next).

Step 1: Certification - USA Baseball

These divisions need a USA Baseball stamp (barrel or taper): Tee Ball (4-6), Kindy (6-8), A (7-9), AA (8-10), AAA (9-11), Majors (10-12)

Tee Ball bats also need "ONLY FOR USE WITH APPROVED TEE BALLS"; 26" max.

Step 1: Certification - BBCOR

These divisions and high school need a BBCOR stamp (barrel or taper): Intermediate (11-13), Juniors (12-14), Seniors (13-16), High School (14+)

Step 1: Certification - USSSA

USSSA (aka "U-dub") is for travel, select and club ball, not Little League. Look for the stamp above on the barrel or taper:

8U, 9U, 10U, 11U, 12U, 13U, 14U+

Step 1: Certification - USA Softball

These softball divisions need a USA Softball (ASA) stamp (barrel or taper): A (7-9), AA (9-12), AAA (9-12), Majors (9-12), Juniors (12-14), Seniors (13-16)

Step 2: Construction

  • One-Piece: Single piece - more balance, more vibration. Good for beginners.
  • Two-Piece: Barrel + handle fused - less vibration, more flex and whip.

Step 2: Materials

  • Alloy: Durable, no break-in, affordable - smaller sweet spot.
  • Wood: Ash, maple, or birch - classic feel, less power than alloy.
  • Composite: Carbon fiber, larger sweet spot - needs 150-200 break-in hits.
  • Hybrid: Alloy barrel + composite handle - reduced vibration, balanced feel.

Step 3: Size It Quickly

  • Length check: Bat next to player in cleats; around hip height is a good start.
  • Drop check: Younger players often start around -10 to -13.
  • Rule: If it feels heavy, go lighter before going longer.

Batting Helmets

Every batter and every base runner wears a batting helmet, in every game and practice.

Every coach's bag has league helmets, but the whole team shares them. Lice spread, sweat builds up, and a shared helmet rarely fits; a loose one can shift on impact. We strongly recommend your own.

Step 1: The NOCSAE Rule

  • Every batting helmet must carry the NOCSAE stamp. Look for "Meets NOCSAE Standard" on the shell or the inside label.
  • No paint, no tape, no changes to the shell. Even stickers can void its NOCSAE certification, so Little League recommends no changes at all.

Step 2: No Chrome

  • It must be non-glare. Matte or standard gloss is fine; mirrored or chrome is not (Rule 1.16).
  • An umpire who spots a chrome helmet will have it taken out of the game (Rule 4.19(a)). If an illegal helmet is used in a game, the player and the manager are ejected and both sit out the team's next game (Rule 3.01).

Step 3: Face Guard or Not?

  • Softball: cage strongly recommended (most come with one).
  • Baseball: optional, recommended (next panel).
  • Juniors, Seniors: same NOCSAE rule.

Adding a face guard or C-flap? Little League allows it only if the helmet maker says it keeps the NOCSAE certification. Keep the maker's notice in the bag for the umpire.

Baseball Face Shield

A face shield (jaw guard or C-flap) covers the cheek and jaw. It's usually sold separately; buy the one made for your helmet and attach it with a screwdriver. Not required in baseball, but recommended.

Softball Face Guards

A face guard (cage) on the batting helmet is strongly recommended; most softball helmets come with one. On defense, a fielder's mask is recommended for infielders, especially pitchers and third base.

Step 4: Measure

  • Soft tape, about an inch above the eyebrows, just above the ears. No hat.
  • Typical: Tee Ball 19-20½"; AAA/Majors 21½-22⅝" (junior); 50/70 and up 23"+ (senior).
  • Brands differ, so use that maker's chart.
  • Between sizes? Go smaller. Don't buy big "to grow into."

Step 5: Fit Check

  • Snug, with no wobble when your player shakes their head.
  • The front edge sits about one finger-width above the eyebrows.
  • The ear holes line up with the ears.
  • Your player can see clearly straight ahead and to the side.

Step 6: Check It Every Season

  • Look for cracks, loose or missing pads, and a loose chin strap or cage.
  • Replace a damaged helmet; don't repair it.
  • No set number of years. Follow the helmet's label; some list how long it can be used. Replace it sooner if it's cracked, took a hard hit, or no longer fits.
  • The manager has the final say on whether a player's own helmet is safe to use.

Catcher's Gear

The league provides full catcher's gear for every team in every division. Your player doesn't need to buy any.

Want your own? It's optional.

What a Catcher Wears

  • Catcher's helmet or mask with a dangling throat protector
  • Chest protector
  • Leg guards (shin guards)
  • A catcher's mitt in baseball; any mitt or glove in softball.

Throat Guards

It hangs from the catcher's mask and protects the neck.

Umpires always check for the throat guard, and a catcher can't play without one. Coaches keep extras in their bags.

Cups and Warm-ups

  • Boys: a hard protective cup. This is the one catcher item your player must bring.
  • Anyone catching, even during warm-ups or between innings, wears at least a mask, a chest protector and (boys) a cup.

Cleats & the Rest

  • Cleats: molded (rubber) cleats. Athletic shoes work for younger players. Metal spikes only in the older divisions.
  • Soccer cleats are fine. Spring ball is on muddy dirt infields, though, so a pair shared with soccer wears and gets dirty faster.
  • Athletic supporter: every boy. Hard cup: boys who catch.
  • Water bottle: with your player's name on it, every practice and game.

Metal Spikes by Division

  • Not allowed: Majors and younger, baseball and softball. Molded cleats only.
  • Allowed: baseball 50/70, Juniors, Seniors; softball Juniors, Seniors.
  • Never for managers, coaches or umpires.

Removable spikes are OK in the younger divisions if no metal shows once they're off. Rules 1.11 and 9.06.

Cups

  • Every boy must wear an athletic supporter.
  • Boys who catch must wear a hard cup, including warm-ups and the bullpen.
  • Everyone else: not required, but recommended (especially infielders).
  • Younger kids: try the Comfy Cup (youth), soft-edged and easier to wear.

Rule 1.17 and the Safety Code.

Pants & Sliding Shorts

  • Color: wait for your coach.
  • Two styles: knickers (end below the knee, socks show) or full-length. Either is fine; it's personal preference.
  • Sliding shorts pad the hips and thighs when sliding, and most have a pocket that holds the cup in place. Optional.

Jewelry, Casts & Glasses

  • Jewelry that could hurt someone comes off, whatever it's made of. Medical-alert jewelry is OK, and so are hard hair items like beads.
  • Casts aren't allowed in games. Anyone in a cast, coaches too, stays in the dugout.
  • Glasses: safety glasses are a good idea.

Rule 1.11(j)-(k) and the Little League Safety Code, baseball and softball.

Sleeves & Pitchers

  • Sleeves: both about the same length; no ragged or slit sleeves.
  • Uniform: no tape or other-colored material stuck on it.
  • Pitchers: undershirts and arm sleeves one solid color (baseball: not white or gray; softball: not the ball's color). Nothing distracting on the hands or arms.

Rules 1.11 and 1.15.

Optional Extras

  • Bat bag: keeps gear together game to game; lasts for years.
  • Batting gloves, sunglasses: optional.
  • Softball fielder's mask: see Batting Helmets.
  • Name on everything; gloves need a phone number too.
